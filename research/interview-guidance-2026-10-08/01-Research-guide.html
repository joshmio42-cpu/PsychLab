Scope and use

This guide supports fictional educational interviews. It organizes questions and evidence; it does not reproduce the full DSM-5-TR, administer a validated diagnostic test, or establish a diagnosis. Research used public professional guidance and primary assessment references. Source-access limitations are recorded in the companion research notes.

For each checkpoint, distinguish recorded evidence from missing information. The interview's selected scenario label is not proof of a diagnosis, and the number of simulated days does not establish lifetime onset or required duration.

Named measures below are references, separate from these original prompts. Choose the correct age, respondent, language and authorized version before any real administration. A life-stage label cannot establish a tool's precise age eligibility. Young children require caregiver and developmental assessment; a text conversation cannot provide physical examination findings.

The catalogue includes DSM-5-TR options plus two explicitly labeled ICD-11 comparisons: complex PTSD and gaming disorder. Missing history stays unknown. The evidence may support more than one explanation or no disorder.

Broad interview guide

Course and developmental context

Establish the usual baseline, first onset, persistence, episodes and changes across settings. The diary length is not the duration of a disorder. Ask about earlier history and leave unavailable information unknown.

Original interview checkpoints

  1. Main concerns and the person's goals

    What would you most like me to understand, and what would you like help with?

  2. Onset, duration and change from usual functioning

    When did this begin, and how have things changed since then?

  3. Effects on daily life and relationships

    How has this affected your usual activities, responsibilities and relationships?

  4. Low mood, loss of interest and irritability

    How has your mood been, and how much enjoyment have you had in your usual interests?

  5. Distinct changes in energy, sleep need and activity

    Have there been periods when your energy or need for sleep was very different from usual?

  6. Worry, fears, panic and avoidance

    What worries or fears have been taking up your attention, and what do you avoid because of them?

  7. Unwanted thoughts, repetitive acts or urges

    Are there thoughts or repeated actions that feel hard to put aside?

  8. Stressors, loss, trauma-related changes and detachment

    Have any difficult experiences or losses affected how you feel or function? We can stay with what feels manageable to discuss.

  9. Unusual perceptions, beliefs or disorganization

    Have you noticed experiences or changes in your thinking that are hard to explain to other people?

  10. Attention, memory, orientation and sudden confusion

    Have you or others noticed changes in concentration, memory or knowing where you are? How quickly did they start?

  11. Sleep, eating and physical symptoms

    What changes have you noticed in sleep, eating or physical wellbeing?

  12. Medicines, substances and behaviors that are hard to control

    What medicines or substances do you use, and are any habits becoming hard to control or causing difficulties?

  13. Developmental history, learning and age-appropriate expectations

    What was learning, communication and everyday life like earlier on, and what support has helped?

  14. Culture, identity, circumstances and alternative explanations

    How do you understand these experiences, and what circumstances or physical health changes might help explain them?

  15. Current safety, self-harm, harm to others and safeguarding

    Have you felt unsafe, had thoughts of harming yourself or someone else, or needed protection from someone?

  16. Strengths, supports and protective relationships

    What helps you cope, and who can you turn to for support?

  17. Previous care, records and other perspectives

    What support or assessments have you had before, and who else could help clarify the history with your permission?

Other explanations to explore

  • Expected developmental or situational variation without sustained impairment
  • Medical, sleep, medication or substance-related explanations
  • More than one mental health condition, with overlapping symptoms
  • Insufficient information: defer a conclusion and seek focused history

Assessment reference

Broad clinical interview; consider an age-appropriate cross-cutting symptom measure

Organize further inquiry rather than assume a particular disorder. Consider a targeted measure only after identifying a concern.

APA lists adult, parent/guardian 6–17 and self-report 11–17 cross-cutting forms. They are adjuncts, not diagnoses. Toddler/preschool interviews need developmental and caregiver assessment instead. Confirm exact age, language, respondent and tool permissions before administering any measure. No questionnaire responses or scores are inferred from this simulation.

Interpretation and care

An omitted symptom is unknown, not absent. Assess distress, functioning and context without forcing a diagnosis. Sudden confusion, acute medical instability or immediate safety concerns call for urgent professional assessment rather than a checklist score. The text interview cannot establish physical examination or observed mental-state findings.

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)

Catalogue life stages: School-age child · Adolescent · Young adult · Adult · Older adult

Course and developmental context

Look beyond the recent diary: persistent symptoms for six months, signs before age 12, and difficulties in more than one setting.

Original interview checkpoints

  1. Attention during ordinary tasks

    Which tasks are difficult to finish, and what happens when you try?

  2. Activity and impulse control

    When is waiting or slowing down difficult?

  3. Childhood pattern

    What do earlier school reports or caregivers recall before age 12?

  4. Different settings and consequences

    How does this affect responsibilities at home and school or work?

  5. Sleep, health and learning context

    When did sleep, mood or learning difficulties appear compared with the attention problems?

Other explanations to explore

  • Sleep disruption or medical effects
  • Anxiety or depression
  • Specific learning or language difficulties

Assessment reference

Structured developmental ADHD assessment

Combine current examples, childhood records and reports across settings.

No single test establishes ADHD. Age-appropriate ratings assist a qualified assessment; checklist completion is not diagnosis.

Interpretation and care

Use child/caregiver and school accounts together. Recent adult attention loss requires a broader medical and mood review.

Sources

  • NIMH: Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder
  • CDC: Diagnosing ADHD

Autism spectrum disorder

Catalogue life stages: Toddler · Preschool child · School-age child · Adolescent · Young adult · Adult · Older adult

Course and developmental context

Assess an early developmental pattern, including differences that become noticeable only as demands increase; recent simulated events do not establish onset.

Original interview checkpoints

  1. Social communication across contexts

    Which parts of conversation or understanding people take extra effort?

  2. Routines, interests and repetitive patterns

    What happens when a familiar routine changes?

  3. Sensory experiences

    Which sounds, textures or environments are unusually difficult or helpful?

  4. Early development and collateral

    What do caregivers or early records tell us about communication and play?

  5. Strengths, functioning and support

    What works well for you, and what support makes daily life easier?

Other explanations to explore

  • Language or hearing differences
  • ADHD or anxiety
  • Intellectual developmental disability, which can coexist

Assessment reference

Comprehensive developmental autism assessment

Integrate observation, developmental history, communication and adaptive functioning.

Screening identifies a need for evaluation; neither a brief checklist nor fluent text establishes or excludes autism.

Interpretation and care

For toddlers use caregiver history and observed play; do not infer intelligence, empathy or support needs from speech style.

  • NIMH: Autism spectrum disorder
  • CDC: Screening for autism spectrum disorder

Specific learning disorder

Catalogue life stages: School-age child · Adolescent · Young adult · Adult · Older adult

Course and developmental context

School-age difficulties persist despite targeted help for at least six months; adult recognition still requires educational history.

Original interview checkpoints

  1. Specific academic difficulty

    Which reading, writing or number tasks remain hard even with practice?

  2. Response to targeted support

    What teaching or support has been tried, and what changed?

  3. School history

    When were these difficulties first noticed?

  4. Practical impact

    How do these tasks affect school, work or everyday independence?

  5. Learning opportunity and language

    What languages and teaching opportunities were available, and have hearing or vision been assessed?

Other explanations to explore

  • Inadequate educational opportunity or unfamiliar instructional language
  • Hearing or vision impairment
  • Broader developmental disability or ADHD

Assessment reference

Comprehensive psychoeducational assessment

Combine achievement assessment, school records and response to instruction.

Standardized results need developmental and educational interpretation; low grades alone are not a diagnosis.

Interpretation and care

Do not equate learning disability with low intelligence. Interpret assessments in the person's language and educational context.

Sources

  • APA: What are specific learning disorders?

Language disorder

Catalogue life stages: Preschool child · School-age child · Adolescent · Young adult · Adult · Older adult

Course and developmental context

Developmental language difficulties may persist into adulthood; evaluate the early history rather than treating a short conversation as a language test.

Original interview checkpoints

  1. Understanding language

    Which instructions or kinds of explanations are difficult to follow?

  2. Expressing ideas

    When is it hard to find words or tell someone what happened?

  3. Early language development

    How did speech and understanding develop when you were younger?

  4. All languages and hearing

    Which languages do you use, and are difficulties similar in each?

  5. Communication impact

    How does this affect learning, relationships or getting needs met?

Other explanations to explore

  • Hearing impairment
  • Language exposure or multilingual assessment mismatch
  • Autism or broader developmental difficulties

Assessment reference

Speech-language pathology assessment

Use observation, language sampling, informant accounts and suitable standardized measures.

Assessment describes language needs and informs diagnosis; a chatbot transcript is not a standardized language assessment.

Interpretation and care

Multilingualism does not cause language disorder. NIDCD's DLD terminology is related to, but not automatically identical with, every DSM language-disorder presentation.

Intellectual developmental disorder (intellectual disability)

Catalogue life stages: Toddler · Preschool child · School-age child · Adolescent · Young adult · Adult · Older adult

Course and developmental context

Intellectual and adaptive limitations begin during development; acquired decline later in life requires a different evaluation.

Original interview checkpoints

  1. Learning and reasoning history

    What kinds of learning have needed repeated support since childhood?

  2. Practical daily skills

    Which daily activities can you do independently, and where is help useful?

  3. Communication and social understanding

    What helps with understanding information and navigating social situations?

  4. Developmental records

    What earlier assessments or caregiver observations are available?

  5. Strengths and access

    What skills and interests are strengths, and what barriers make tasks harder?

Other explanations to explore

  • Specific learning or language disorder
  • Autism, including possible co-occurrence
  • Acquired neurocognitive decline

Assessment reference

Intellectual and adaptive-functioning assessment

Pair culturally appropriate cognitive evaluation with everyday skills and informant history; adaptive tools may include Vineland scales.

A score alone is insufficient. Qualified interpretation of both functioning domains and developmental onset is required.

Interpretation and care

Do not infer intellectual ability from limited speech. Very young children need developmental assessment; avoid invented IQ scores or support levels.

Sources

Tourette's disorder (Tourette syndrome)

Catalogue life stages: School-age child · Adolescent · Young adult · Adult · Older adult

Course and developmental context

Establish childhood onset before 18 and more than a year since tics began; motor and vocal tics may change or fluctuate.

Original interview checkpoints

  1. Motor and vocal tic history

    What repeated movements or sounds have occurred, even if they are absent today?

  2. Age at onset and changing course

    When did the first tic appear, and how has the pattern changed?

  3. Experience around tics

    What do you notice before a tic or when trying to hold it back?

  4. Pain and participation

    Do tics affect comfort, school, work or interactions?

  5. Medical and medication context

    What medicines, illnesses or other movements need to be considered?

Other explanations to explore

  • Persistent motor or vocal tic disorder
  • Provisional tic disorder
  • Functional tic-like symptoms or neurological/medication effects

Assessment reference

Clinical tic and developmental assessment

Review tic types, chronology, observation and possible alternative causes.

A clinician interprets the pattern; text descriptions cannot substitute for examining movement symptoms.

Interpretation and care

Swearing is not required. Lack of impairment does not by itself exclude Tourette's; sudden new tic-like symptoms need appropriate assessment.

Major depressive disorder

Catalogue life stages: Adolescent · Young adult · Adult · Older adult

Course and developmental context

Explore a depressive episode lasting at least two weeks alongside the lifetime mood history; diary length alone cannot establish an episode.

Original interview checkpoints

  1. Mood and loss of enjoyment

    How have your mood and enjoyment changed from your usual self?

  2. Episode continuity

    When did this change begin, and has it been present most days?

  3. Sleep, energy and thinking

    What has changed in sleep, appetite, energy or concentration?

  4. Daily functioning

    Which responsibilities or relationships have become difficult?

  5. Safety and hopelessness

    Have you had thoughts about dying or harming yourself, and do you feel safe now?

  6. Past elevation and other explanations

    Have there been separate periods of unusually high energy with little need for sleep, or health or substance changes?

Other explanations to explore

  • Bipolar depression
  • Medical or substance effects
  • Grief or adjustment-related distress

Assessment reference

APA depression severity measure (adapted PHQ-9)

Record recent adult depressive symptom burden alongside an interview.

This linked adaptation uses seven days. It supports severity assessment, not definitive diagnosis; use an appropriate adolescent version for youth.

Interpretation and care

Adolescents may describe irritability. Safety concerns require direct assessment regardless of any score.

Sources

  • NIMH: Depression
  • APA: Adult depression severity measure, adapted PHQ-9

Persistent depressive disorder

Catalogue life stages: Adolescent · Young adult · Adult · Older adult

Course and developmental context

Chronic depressed mood usually spans at least two years in adults, or one year in children/adolescents; investigate better intervals and superimposed episodes.

Original interview checkpoints

  1. Long-term mood baseline

    How long has feeling low been part of your usual life?

  2. Periods of improvement

    What was the longest time you felt more like yourself?

  3. Energy and self-view

    Over those years, how have energy, confidence and hope changed?

  4. More intense episodes

    Were there separate stretches when things became substantially worse or unusually energized?

  5. Functioning and safety

    What has this cost you in daily life, and have you felt unsafe or wished not to be alive?

Other explanations to explore

  • Recurrent major depressive episodes
  • Bipolar spectrum illness
  • Medical/substance effects or continuing adversity

Assessment reference

Longitudinal mood assessment

Build a timeline using the person's history and available collateral.

A short symptom scale can describe current severity but cannot prove a chronic course or definitively diagnose PDD.

Interpretation and care

Do not describe every chronic depression as mild. Review safety and the possibility of major episodes; a 28-day diary cannot supply years of evidence.

Sources

  • APA: Depression and related conditions
  • NIMH: Depression

Disruptive mood dysregulation disorder (DMDD)

Catalogue life stages: School-age child · Adolescent

Course and developmental context

Review at least a year of persistent irritability and frequent severe outbursts; APA specifies onset before 10 and first diagnosis between 6 and 18.

Original interview checkpoints

  1. Outburst frequency and context

    Walk me through a recent outburst and what happened just before it.

  2. Mood between outbursts

    What is mood like on an ordinary day between these episodes?

  3. Onset and longer well periods

    When did this start, and were there several months when it stopped?

  4. Home, school and peer settings

    What do caregivers, teachers and the child each notice?

  5. Distinct high-energy episodes

    Has there been a separate period of unusually high energy and much less need for sleep?

Other explanations to explore

  • Episodic bipolar mood disorder
  • Oppositional defiant disorder
  • ADHD, anxiety or developmental/contextual difficulties

Assessment reference

Affective Reactivity Index (ARI), age-appropriate form

Describe irritability with youth and caregiver information.

ARI assesses irritability, not DMDD specifically. The linked parent adaptation is for ages 6–17 and a seven-day interval; it cannot prove the year-long course.

Interpretation and care

Use both the child's account and collateral. APA's age rule is more precise than NIMH's simplified 6–10 wording; do not automatically add ODD when DMDD criteria are met.

Sources

  • APA: Disruptive mood dysregulation disorder fact sheet
  • NIMH: DMDD — the basics
  • APA: Parent ARI irritability measure, ages 6–17

Premenstrual dysphoric disorder

Catalogue life stages: Adolescent · Young adult · Adult

Course and developmental context

Establish repeated mood and functional changes before menstruation with improvement afterwards; use prospective daily records across at least two menstrual cycles to clarify the pattern.

Original interview checkpoints

  1. Relationship to menstruation

    Where in your cycle do these changes begin and ease?

  2. Specific emotional changes

    What changes in mood, tension or irritability stand out then?

  3. Symptoms outside that phase

    How do you feel during the rest of the cycle?

  4. Recurring impact

    What becomes difficult at school, work or in relationships during those days?

  5. Daily records and other causes

    What daily records, medicines or ongoing mood difficulties help explain the pattern?

Other explanations to explore

  • Premenstrual worsening of another disorder
  • PMS without comparable impairment
  • Medication or other health effects

Assessment reference

Daily Record of Severity of Problems (DRSP)

Prospectively track symptoms and impairment across menstrual phases.

The published measure supports clinical assessment; retrospective recall or a single daily score cannot establish PMDD. No DRSP scoring is implemented here.

Interpretation and care

Do not infer menstruation from name, gender or life-stage label. The DRSP validation cited used adults and DSM-IV-era definitions; current clinical interpretation remains necessary.

Bipolar I disorder

Catalogue life stages: Adolescent · Young adult · Adult · Older adult

Course and developmental context

A manic episode is central: typically at least seven days, or a shorter severe episode requiring hospitalization. Depression may occur but is not required.

Original interview checkpoints

  1. Distinct change from usual mood and energy

    Describe the clearest period when your energy and behavior were unlike your usual self.

  2. Sleep need and activity

    How much sleep did you need, and what were you doing during that time?

  3. Episode duration and recovery

    How long did that period last, and what happened afterwards?

  4. Consequences and loss of control

    Did it disrupt responsibilities, require hospital care or involve experiences others did not share?

  5. Substances, health and collateral

    What medicines or substances were involved, and what did people close to you observe?

Other explanations to explore

  • Substance/medication-related activation
  • Medical causes such as thyroid illness
  • Psychotic disorders; compare mood and psychosis chronology

Assessment reference

Specialist longitudinal mood assessment

Reconstruct mania, depression, baseline functioning and collateral accounts.

Symptom ratings support assessment but cannot independently establish bipolar I. Severe mania or psychosis needs urgent clinical assessment.

Interpretation and care

Ordinary mood changes are insufficient. Assess safety, self-care and impaired judgment directly; do not assume dangerousness from the label.

Sources

  • NIMH: Bipolar disorder
  • APA: What are bipolar disorders?

Bipolar II disorder

Catalogue life stages: Adolescent · Young adult · Adult · Older adult

Course and developmental context

Establish both hypomanic and major depressive episodes, without a manic episode; the hypomanic duration anchor is four consecutive days.

Original interview checkpoints

  1. Depressive episodes

    Which low periods most affected your daily life, and how long did they last?

  2. Distinct increased-energy periods

    Were there separate stretches of increased energy that others noticed?

  3. Sleep need and duration

    During those stretches, what happened to sleep, and how many days did the change continue?

  4. History of more severe elevation

    Has any energized period caused major disruption, hospital care or psychosis?

  5. Baseline and possible causes

    How did you function between episodes, and were medicines or substances involved?

Other explanations to explore

  • Major depressive disorder with missed hypomania
  • Bipolar I if mania has occurred
  • Substance effects or chronic attention difficulties

Assessment reference

Specialist episode-history assessment

Review both depressive and elevated periods with corroborating history.

No brief depression or mania scale definitively distinguishes bipolar II. A history consistent with mania changes the formulation.

Interpretation and care

Hypomania may be remembered as productive, so ask about observable change. Bipolar II can involve severe depression; review safety separately.

  • NIMH: Bipolar disorder

Cyclothymic disorder

Catalogue life stages: Adolescent · Young adult · Adult · Older adult

Course and developmental context

Review a fluctuating subthreshold mood pattern across two years in adults or one year in youth; brief diary changes are insufficient.

Original interview checkpoints

  1. Recurring energized periods

    What do your more energized stretches look like?

  2. Recurring low periods

    How are your lower stretches different from your usual mood?

  3. Timeline and gaps

    Across the longer history, how long have the better intervals lasted?

  4. Possible full mood episodes

    Has any period been severe or sustained enough to cause major disruption or require treatment?

  5. Consequences and other causes

    How has the pattern affected relationships or responsibilities, and what else could explain it?

Other explanations to explore

  • Bipolar I or II with full episodes
  • Substance or medication effects
  • Stress-related mood changes

Assessment reference

Longitudinal mood timeline with clinical assessment

Compare symptom periods, gaps, functional effects and full-episode history.

Mood journals assist assessment but are not a definitive diagnosis; a short observation window cannot establish cyclothymia.

Interpretation and care

Do not equate hourly emotional shifts with cyclothymia. Subthreshold symptoms can still impair life; assess safety and changes suggesting a full episode.

  • APA: What are bipolar disorders?

Generalized anxiety disorder

Catalogue life stages: School-age child · Adolescent · Young adult · Adult · Older adult

Course and developmental context

Explore difficult-to-control worry across several areas on most days for at least six months, with age-appropriate associated features and impairment.

Original interview checkpoints

  1. Breadth of worry

    What subjects keep coming back in your worries?

  2. Difficulty disengaging

    What happens when you try to put the worries aside?

  3. Persistence

    When did this pattern start, and how often is it present?

  4. Tension, sleep and concentration

    How does worry affect your body, sleep or concentration?

  5. Consequences and alternatives

    What does worry stop you doing, and what health or substance changes overlap with it?

Other explanations to explore

  • Ordinary stress or adjustment difficulty
  • Other anxiety disorders with narrower triggers
  • Medical or substance-related symptoms

Assessment reference

APA Generalized Anxiety Disorder Severity Measure—Adult

Assess recent anxiety burden after symptoms have been identified.

A severity aid, not a definitive diagnostic test; it cannot establish six-month duration. Use an age-appropriate youth assessment for children.

Interpretation and care

Do not apply an adult symptom-count rule to children. Check whether worry concerns actual ongoing danger and obtain caregiver context when appropriate.

Sources

  • NIMH: Generalized anxiety disorder
  • APA: Adult generalized anxiety severity measure
  • AACAP: Anxiety disorder frequently asked questions

Panic disorder

Catalogue life stages: Adolescent · Young adult · Adult · Older adult

Course and developmental context

Recurrent unexpected panic attacks must be distinguished from isolated attacks; examine at least a month of continuing concern or related behavior changes.

Original interview checkpoints

  1. Description of an attack

    Tell me what happened during the most recent sudden surge of fear.

  2. Unexpected versus cue-linked attacks

    Were attacks surprising, or did they occur around a particular fear?

  3. Worry and changed behavior

    Since the attacks, what have you worried about or stopped doing?

  4. Recurrence and duration

    When did attacks begin, and how long have the changes afterwards continued?

  5. Health and substance review

    What medical assessment or medicine and substance changes are relevant?

Other explanations to explore

  • Panic attacks within another disorder
  • Cardiac, respiratory or endocrine causes
  • Substance or medication effects

Assessment reference

APA Panic Disorder Severity Measure—Adult

Describe recent attacks, fear and avoidance as part of assessment.

Measures symptom burden, not a definitive diagnosis. A raised score does not establish unexpected attacks or exclude medical causes.

Interpretation and care

New severe chest symptoms, fainting or breathing difficulty require medical assessment rather than an assumption of panic. Use appropriate youth evaluation for adolescents.

Sources

  • NIMH: Panic disorder
  • APA: Adult panic disorder severity measure

Social anxiety disorder

Catalogue life stages: School-age child · Adolescent · Young adult · Adult · Older adult

Course and developmental context

Assess persistent fear of negative evaluation with avoidance or marked distress and functional effects, generally across at least six months.

Original interview checkpoints

  1. Feared social outcome

    What do you fear other people might notice or think?

  2. Situations and breadth

    Which social or performance situations are difficult, and which feel comfortable?

  3. Avoidance and endurance

    What do you do to get through or avoid those situations?

  4. Persistence and development

    When did this pattern begin, including around peers when you were younger?

  5. Lost opportunities and context

    How has this affected friendships, learning or work, and are bullying or communication differences involved?

Other explanations to explore

  • Ordinary shyness without impairment
  • Autism or communication differences
  • Body-image preoccupation or broader anxiety

Assessment reference

APA Social Anxiety Disorder Severity Measure—Adult

Describe recent fear, avoidance and interference.

A severity assessment aid, not a definitive diagnosis. Use developmentally appropriate interviewing or a suitable youth form for children.

Interpretation and care

Context and actual discrimination matter. Do not diagnose from quietness alone or assume childhood anxiety only around unfamiliar adults meets the pattern.

Sources

  • NIMH: Social anxiety disorder
  • APA: Adult social anxiety severity measure

Specific phobia

Catalogue life stages: School-age child · Adolescent · Young adult · Adult · Older adult

Course and developmental context

Explore persistent fear tied to a particular object or situation, generally for six months or more, with distress or interference; distinguish ordinary developmental fear and actual danger.

Original interview checkpoints

  1. Specific feared trigger

    What particular object or situation brings the fear on?

  2. Response when exposed

    What happens in your body and thoughts when it is nearby?

  3. Avoidance

    How have you arranged life to avoid it?

  4. Persistence across time

    How long has this fear lasted, and has it changed?

  5. Impact and realistic threat

    What important activity is affected, and what risk is actually present?

Other explanations to explore

  • Developmentally ordinary fears
  • Trauma reminders or obsessive fears
  • Agoraphobia or social-evaluation fears

Assessment reference

APA Specific Phobia Severity Measure—Adult

Describe recent fear and avoidance for an identified trigger.

Severity assessment, not definitive diagnosis; it does not establish the full diagnostic course. Use appropriate child assessment for younger patients.

Interpretation and care

Do not provoke exposure during a diagnostic chat. Avoid classifying reasonable caution in dangerous settings as illness.

    What tends to happen when an important relationship feels uncertain?

  2. Emotion changes and recovery

    How do strong feelings build and settle, and what tends to set them off?

  3. Self-image and direction

    Does your sense of who you are or what matters to you change at difficult times?

  4. Coping, impulsivity and safety

    When overwhelmed, what do you do to cope, and have you felt at risk of harming yourself?

  5. Pattern versus mood episode

    Has this pattern appeared for years, or mainly in distinct episodes of unusually high energy or depression?

  • Bipolar mood episodes
  • Trauma-related conditions
  • Depressive or substance-use disorder

Use nonjudgmental language and assess safety directly. Adolescent diagnosis needs persistent evidence and specialist developmental assessment; do not call distress manipulative.

Gender dysphoria

Catalogue life stages: Adolescent · Young adult · Adult · Older adult

Course and developmental context

Assess persistence, distress and functioning over at least six months using developmentally appropriate child or adolescent/adult criteria.

Original interview checkpoints

  1. Person's own language and goals

    What name and language feel right for you, and what would you like help with?

  2. Specific distress rather than identity

    Which experiences with your body or how others see you cause distress, if any?

  3. Developmental timing

    When did these concerns become important, and how have they changed with development?

  4. Function and social context

    How does this affect daily life, and what part comes from how other people treat you?

  5. Safety, strengths and support

    Where do you feel accepted and safe, and what support would help?

Other explanations to explore

  • Gender diversity without clinically significant dysphoria
  • Distress related to discrimination or rejection
  • Co-occurring depression, anxiety or body-image concerns

Assessment reference

Developmentally appropriate clinical assessment of gender-related distress

Clarify the person's experience, persistence, impairment and support needs.

No identity test establishes a disorder. Evaluate distress in context; this checklist cannot diagnose or determine a treatment pathway.

Interpretation and care

Transgender or gender-diverse identity is not itself a disorder. Pronouns, clothing or interests do not establish dysphoria. Avoid assumptions about puberty, anatomy or desired outcomes.

Complex PTSD — ICD-11 comparison

Catalogue life stages: Young adult · Adult · Older adult

Course and developmental context

Use ICD-11: establish trauma-related PTSD features plus persistent difficulties in emotion regulation, self-view and relationships, with functional impact.

Original interview checkpoints

  1. Trauma context and permission

    Without details you do not want to share, what experience still affects you?

  2. Re-experiencing, avoidance and threat

    What reminders bring the experience into the present, and what do you avoid or stay alert for?

  3. Regulating emotion

    What happens when feelings become intense or shut down?

  4. Self-view and relationships

    How has this affected how you see yourself and feel close to people?

  5. Persistence and impairment

    How long have these difficulties affected daily life, and what still helps?

Other explanations to explore

  • ICD-11 PTSD without additional self-organization difficulties
  • Borderline personality disorder
  • Depression or dissociative conditions

Assessment reference

International Trauma Questionnaire (ITQ)

Self-report assessment of ICD-11 PTSD and complex PTSD features and impairment.

Supports a provisional formulation, not a definitive diagnosis. Use an appropriate age/language version; no ITQ score is derived from these practice prompts.

Interpretation and care

CPTSD is a separate ICD-11 diagnosis, not a separate DSM-5-TR diagnosis. Repeated trauma alone is insufficient; avoid forcing a detailed trauma narrative.

Gaming disorder — ICD-11 comparison

Catalogue life stages: Adolescent · Young adult · Adult · Older adult

Course and developmental context

ICD-11 normally requires an impairing pattern over at least 12 months; use the full ICD-11 requirements when considering exceptions.

Original interview checkpoints

  1. Control of gaming

    What happens when you plan to end a gaming session?

  2. Competing priorities

    Which activities or responsibilities have gaming displaced?

  3. Continuation despite harm

    What problems have you noticed, and how has gaming changed since then?

  4. Duration and functional impact

    For how long has this pattern affected school, work, relationships or health?

  5. Context and alternative explanations

    What do gaming and your online community provide, and what else is affecting your life?

Other explanations to explore

  • High engagement without significant impairment
  • Gaming as coping with depression or anxiety
  • Mania-related change in activity
  • Gambling behavior involving bets or stakes

Assessment reference

Structured ICD-11 gaming assessment

Assess control, priority, persistence despite consequences and significant impairment.

Hours played alone are not diagnostic. This is an educational interview framework, not a validated scored instrument.

Interpretation and care

Keep ICD-11 gaming disorder distinct from DSM-5-TR Internet Gaming Disorder, a condition for further study. Interpret youth routines and family conflict developmentally.
